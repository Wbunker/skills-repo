## Choose the method by the question

Match the method to the question actually being asked, not to habit or budget.

| Question | Method |
|---|---|
| "Why do people buy, switch, or churn?" | Switch interviews |
| "What does 'better' mean, measurably, and what should we build next?" | Job mapping + outcome survey (the ODI arc) |
| "How do we frame this backlog or design?" | Job stories drafted from existing evidence |
| "Why are demos and deals stalling?" | Call-archive mining + forces-based win/loss debriefs (references/artifact-analysis.md) |

Rule of thumb: interview before you survey. A survey can only rank the statements you feed it, so outcome statements you did not ground qualitatively measure your imagination, not the market — precise-looking scores for the wrong list.

## The switch interview

Reconstruct the true story of one real purchase, cancellation, or solution change in documentary detail (Moesta/Re-Wired style). The unit of analysis is the decision, not the person.

### Recruit real, recent switchers

- Interview only people who actually switched — bought, quit, or changed solutions — within roughly the last 90 days.
- Demand real behavior because intentions and hypotheticals are confabulated. People predict themselves poorly but narrate concrete past events reliably once anchored in specific detail; a real decision has a timeline that must stay internally consistent, while a hypothetical has nothing to push against.
- Keep it recent because episodic memory decays fast. Much past 90 days the story compresses into a rationalized summary ("we just needed better reporting") and the causal texture — the deadline, the outage, the colleague's remark — is gone.
- Plan 8–12 interviews per job; the recurring pushes, anxieties, and trade-offs of its story patterns typically saturate in that range. One story is an anecdote: a lead to chase, never a finding.
- Recruit from records of behavior, not self-description: recent closed-won deals, recent cancellations, recent trial conversions. Screen out anyone "considering a switch" — considering is not switching.
- Pay for their time. Compensation improves show rates and does not distort a reconstructed story the way it distorts opinions.
- In B2B, start with the person who lived the struggle and drove the change; bring in other players (approver, blocker, end users) as the story names them.

### Set the frame

Open by telling the interviewee what kind of conversation this is, because the framing selects which memory system they use:

- "I'm going to ask about specific moments. 'I don't remember' is a fine answer — we'll work back to it."
- "There are no wrong answers, and I'm not selling anything. I want the story of what actually happened."
- Permission to remember slowly keeps people out of performance mode, where they optimize for sounding decisive instead of being accurate.

### Run it as documentary reconstruction

You are making a documentary about a decision that already happened, not administering a survey.

- Build the timeline BACKWARD from the moment of purchase or cancellation. Open at the concrete endpoint ("Take me to the day you signed up — what day of the week was it?") and rewind toward the First Thought. Working backward keeps the interviewee narrating events instead of skipping to a rehearsed conclusion.
- Chase concrete episodic anchors: what day was it, where were you, who else was there, what else was going on that week. Specific sensory context unlocks true memory; "why did you…" invites rationalization — the press-release version of the decision.
- Ask "what happened next?" and "what was going on then?" rather than "why". Extract causation from the reconstructed sequence; never request it as testimony, because a "why" answer is manufactured on the spot to sound reasonable.
- Cover one decision per session. If they have switched twice, pick the most recent and stay on it; blended stories produce blended forces.
- When the interviewee drifts into present-tense generalities ("what I usually do is…"), rewind to a specific instance: "Take me to the last time that happened."
- Treat "I don't remember" as a cue for an anchor, not a dead end. "Was it before or after the holidays?" routinely recovers the scene.

### Listen for and timestamp

Pin each of these to a date or triggering event before the interview ends:

- The First Thought — when the idea that something had to change was planted, and by what: an event, a remark, a bill, a failure.
- The passive→active transition — the event that turned idle noticing (Passive Looking) into spending real time and effort (Active Looking).
- The deciding trade-offs — "What almost stopped you?" is the highest-yield question in the method: its answer names the anxieties and habits your marketing and onboarding must defuse.
- The four forces wherever they surface: push of the situation, pull of the new solution, anxiety of the new solution, habit of the present.

If one is still missing when time runs short, ask for it directly — a blunt question late beats a hole in the timeline.

### Logistics

- Book 45–60 minutes. Shorter cannot reach the early timeline; longer exhausts scene-recall.
- Use two interviewers when possible: one drives the conversation, one tracks the timeline and the four forces so nothing gets skipped. The driver stays present with the storyteller; the tracker guards completeness.
- Record and transcribe. The analysis below depends on verbatim quotes, and live note-taking flattens language exactly where wording matters most.
- Bring energy and genuine curiosity. The storyteller mirrors your affect: flat administration gets flat summaries, while visible fascination keeps them in scene-recall mode.
- Spend most of the hour before the moment of purchase. The decision was made in the first two-thirds of the timeline; the demo stage rarely contains the cause.
- Debrief within a day: the two interviewers reconcile the timeline and forces notes while the story is still fresh.

### Interviewer moves

| Move | Say | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Rewind | "Take me back to the moment you…" | Re-enters the scene instead of the summary |
| Camera check | "Where were you standing? Who else was there?" | Sensory detail unlocks adjacent true memories |
| Contrast probe | "What were you using the week before?" | Surfaces the incumbent and the habit of the present |
| Force probe | "What almost stopped you?" | Names anxieties and end-stage trade-offs directly |
| Fire probe | "What did the old solution do that finally made you done with it?" | Locates the push of the situation as an event, not an opinion |

## Question bank by timeline stage

Adapt phrasing to the domain, but keep every question pointed at events that already happened. Three to five per stage is enough; the real follow-ups come from the story.

**First Thought**
- "When did it first cross your mind that something had to change? What was happening that day?"
- "Who or what put the idea in your head — a person, an event, a bill, a breakdown?"
- "What were you using at the time, and how long had that been true?"
- "When the thought came up, what did you do about it, if anything?"

**Passive Looking**
- "What did you start noticing after that? Who did you mention it to?"
- "What did you read, click, or bookmark without really acting on it?"
- "How long did that just-noticing stretch last — weeks? months?"
- "What kept it from feeling urgent yet?"

**Active Looking**
- "What was the first thing you did that took real time?"
- "What happened that made you start looking seriously right then?"
- "What did you compare? What got eliminated and why?"
- "Who else got pulled in once you were seriously looking?"
- "What did you try before shopping for something new — workarounds, doing it by hand, doing nothing?"

**Deciding**
- "What were you weighing right at the end?"
- "What almost stopped you?"
- "What did you give up by choosing this?"
- "Was there a deadline or event that forced the timing?"

**Onboarding**
- "Walk me through the first use. What surprised you against what you expected?"
- "What did you do in the first hour? The first week?"
- "Was there a moment you wondered whether you'd made a mistake?"
- "Who else had to start using it, and how did that go?"

**Ongoing Use**
- "What do you use it for now that you didn't expect?"
- "What still makes you grit your teeth?"
- "What would have to happen for you to stop?"
- "What do you miss about the old solution, honestly?"

## From transcripts to findings

### Per interviewee

- Reconstruct the dated timeline: First Thought → Passive Looking → Active Looking → Deciding → Onboarding → Ongoing Use, each transition pinned to its triggering event.
- Fill a four-forces evidence table: verbatim quote → force → interpretation. Verbatim first, because analysis must stay traceable to what was actually said, and because the wording ("I was done", "I just couldn't risk it") often carries the force more precisely than any paraphrase.
- Tag each quote with its timeline stage as well as its force, so synthesis can see when in the journey each force did its work.
- Mark gaps honestly. A timeline with an unexplained jump ("then suddenly they were in a demo") is a probe list for the next interview, not something to smooth over.

| Verbatim quote | Force | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| "The third time the export failed during board prep, I was done." | Push of the situation | A failure during a high-stakes moment, not the feature gap itself, triggered the switch |

### Across interviewees

- Cluster struggling moments and circumstances, then draft job statements: verb + object + contextual clarifier (grammar in references/formats.md).
- Keep the circumstance attached to every cluster — a struggling moment stripped of its circumstance cannot supply the contextual clarifier, and the job statement degrades into a generic verb phrase.
- Promote recurring "so I can…" clauses into desired outcome statements: direction of improvement + performance metric + object of control + contextual clarifier.
- Note which forces recur as the dominant blockers. That finding usually changes marketing and onboarding before it changes product: recurring anxiety of the new solution calls for proof and risk-reversal, recurring habit of the present calls for migration paths — neither is a feature request.
- Stop when new interviews stop producing new patterns; if interview nine still surprises you, keep going.

### Deliverable

- Per-interview timeline summaries.
- A cross-interview forces table showing which forces recur, in which circumstances, at which stages.
- Drafted job statements and desired outcome statements, each traceable to supporting quotes and marked [Stated] (in the interviewee's own words) or [Inferred] (your synthesis). The marks keep later readers from mistaking your interpretation for their testimony.

## The ODI quantitative arc

Outcome-Driven Innovation (Ulwick) quantifies a job after qualitative work has defined it. Run the steps in order — each exists to make the next one valid.

**1. Define the job and build the job map.**
Interview job performers until you can state the core functional job and lay out its steps: Define, Locate, Prepare, Confirm, Execute, Monitor, Modify, Conclude.
Why: the map forces coverage — without it, outcome gathering clusters around the vivid middle (Execute) and misses the planning and wrap-up steps where underserved outcomes hide.

**2. Generate the desired-outcome set.**
Extract 50–150 desired outcome statements (commonly 75+) from the interviews, in strict outcome grammar (references/formats.md): one metric, one direction, no solutions, no ambiguity.
Why: each statement must be individually surveyable; sloppy grammar here poisons every downstream number.

**3. Survey a representative sample of job performers.**
Have each respondent rate every outcome's importance and current satisfaction; compute both as top-2-box percentages expressed on a 0–10 basis (73% top-2-box → 7.3).
Why: top-2-box rewards consensus — an outcome half the market rates critical and half ignores should not masquerade as moderately important.
Note: job performers rate satisfaction with however they get the job done today — including spreadsheets and workarounds — not satisfaction with your product.

**4. Score opportunity.**
Opportunity = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0), on a 0–20 scale. ≥10 is attractive; ≥12 is strongly underserved; satisfaction above importance is overserved; both high is table stakes. Detail in references/formats.md.
Why: the formula surfaces outcomes that are both important and unsatisfied — important-but-satisfied is defended ground, unimportant-and-unsatisfied is noise.

**5. Segment by outcomes.**
Cluster respondents by their patterns of unmet outcomes, then describe each segment by circumstance + jobs + unmet outcomes — never demographics.
Why: demographics feel familiar but do not predict struggle; two same-age managers at similar firms can sit in opposite segments, and a demographic label would hide it.

**6. Target and aim the roadmap.**
Choose the segments and underserved outcomes you can serve profitably, then evaluate every roadmap candidate by which targeted outcomes it moves and by how much.
Why: this is the payoff — a roadmap argued from measured unmet outcomes instead of stakeholder volume.

When ODI is worth it: a mature market, real roadmap stakes, and hundreds of reachable job performers.
When it is overkill: an early-stage product still hunting for the struggling moment — run switch interviews and forces work first, because you cannot write 75 surveyable outcomes for a job you have not yet heard described.

Honest caveat: below roughly 180 usable respondents, treat opportunity scores as ordinal ranks, not precise measurements — the percentages behind them are too noisy to support decimal-level comparisons.

## Mining what already exists

Call recordings, demo archives, support logs, reviews, and win/loss notes are switch-story fossils: pushes, anxieties, and deciding trade-offs narrated in real time, with no recall decay. Analyze them with the playbooks in references/artifact-analysis.md before commissioning new research — a week in the archive often answers the question a study was about to be scoped for.

- Sales call and demo recordings: pushes and anxieties verbatim, plus the exact questions that stall deals.
- Support logs and tickets: Ongoing Use struggling moments, and the workarounds that signal unmet outcomes.
- Reviews of your product and competitors': compressed switch stories — "moved from X because…" is a miniature timeline.
- Win/loss and churn notes: the Deciding stage, told from both endings.

Give sales teams a forces-based win/loss debrief — five questions asked about every closed or dead deal:

1. What was breaking for them? (push of the situation)
2. What else did they look at? (pull of the new solution, and the real competitive set)
3. What almost stopped the deal? (anxiety of the new solution)
4. What were they giving up? (habit of the present, and the switching costs)
5. Who else had to be convinced, and of what?

The set works because each question maps to a force, so aggregated debriefs become a cross-deal forces table with no extra analysis step.

## Pitfalls

- **Pitching during an interview.** You stop getting truth the moment you defend the product; the interviewee starts managing your feelings instead of remembering.
  Log the objection silently and keep filming the documentary.
- **Asking "what do you want?"** Invites feature invention, which people are bad at.
  Ask what happened instead; wants are inferred from struggles, not collected.
- **Future hypotheticals.** "Would you use…?" is worthless — the answer costs nothing and predicts nothing.
  Ask about the last time they faced the situation.
- **Recruiting by persona instead of recent switch behavior.** Personas describe who people are; switching reveals what they were trying to get done.
  Recruit the behavior, then let the stories define the segments.
- **Averaging across circumstances.** The mean of two different struggles describes nobody.
  Segment by circumstance and outcomes before summarizing anything.
- **Stopping at the functional dimension.** Emotional and social criteria often decide the hire even when the functional story sounds complete.
  Probe how they wanted to feel and how they wanted to be seen.
- **Treating one vivid story as the market.** One story is an anecdote, however cinematic.
  Keep interviewing until patterns saturate, typically 8–12.
- **Leading the witness with your job hypothesis.** Questions shaped to confirm a hypothesis will confirm it.
  Hold it loosely and hunt for disconfirmation.
