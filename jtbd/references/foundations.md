## Lineage

Know the lineage because every JTBD source you meet belongs to a school, and the school determines what its advice is good for. When two sources conflict, the conflict is usually genealogical, not factual.

**Theodore Levitt** is the intellectual ancestor. His marketing dictum — "people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole" — is the whole framework in one sentence: demand attaches to the outcome, not the product. Everything after Levitt is machinery for finding the hole.

**Tony Ulwick** built the first systematic machinery. At IBM in the 1980s he worked on the PCjr, a product built faithfully to internal specs that failed in the market — the lesson he took was that products fail when they are built to specifications instead of to customer-defined metrics of success. He founded Strategyn and through the 1990s developed the precursor to Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI). The classic early case is his work with Cordis Corporation on angioplasty balloons: interviewing clinicians for measurable desired outcomes rather than feature requests reordered the roadmap and produced category-leading products. His 2002 Harvard Business Review article "Turn Customer Input into Innovation" brought outcomes language to a wide audience.

**Clayton Christensen** named the field. In *The Innovator's Solution* (2003) he introduced "jobs to be done" as the answer to why disruption theory's market-segmentation advice kept failing, illustrated by the milkshake study conducted with **Bob Moesta**. *Competing Against Luck* (2016) is the full statement of his theory of jobs as progress in a circumstance.

**Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek** (Re-Wired Group) built the applied practice: the switch interview, the four forces, and the demand-side timeline that traces a purchase from first thought to ongoing use. Moesta later wrote *Demand-Side Sales 101*, which recasts selling as helping people make progress through that timeline.

**Alan Klement** (*When Coffee and Kale Compete*) is the sharpest theorist of the jobs-as-progress position — jobs as the struggle to transform a life-situation, never containing solutions. The **job story** format ("When ... I want to ... so I can ...") came out of Intercom's product practice and Klement's 2013 article "Replacing The User Story With The Job Story"; Intercom's free book *Intercom on Jobs-to-be-Done* spread JTBD through the product and UX world.

**Jim Kalbach** (*The Jobs To Be Done Playbook*) synthesized both schools into a practitioner's toolkit and popularized the neutral term **job performer**, which this skill uses as its default.

When adjudicating between sources, place the author first:

- Ulwick / Strategyn material → jobs-as-activities; trust it for method, metrics, and prioritization mechanics.
- Christensen / Moesta / Re-Wired material → jobs-as-progress; trust it for the causality of switching, sales, and marketing.
- Klement → the strictest progress theory; trust it for definitional rigor and for stress-testing job statements.
- Intercom → applied product/UX pragmatics; light on theory, strong on putting job stories into team workflows.
- Kalbach → synthesis and facilitation formats; the bridge vocabulary this skill defaults to.

## The milkshake study

This is the field's load-bearing example. Tell it with its actual logic, because the logic is the lesson.

A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. It did the orthodox things: segmented customers by demographics, profiled the likeliest milkshake buyers, asked them how to improve the product (thicker? chunkier? cheaper? more chocolate?), and shipped the improvements. Sales did not move.

Moesta's team instead observed the circumstances of purchase. Roughly half of all milkshakes sold before 8 a.m., to customers who were alone, bought nothing else, and drove off with the shake. Interviews revealed the job: a long, boring commute, one free hand, and the need to stay occupied and unhungry until mid-morning. The milkshake was hired because it fit the circumstance — it occupied the free hand, was thick enough to last the whole drive through a thin straw, was cleaner than a bagel with cream cheese, and was more filling than a banana. The competitors were bananas, bagels, donuts, and boredom — not other milkshakes, and not other chains.

The afternoon milkshake was a different job entirely: a parent saying yes to a child, hiring the shake to placate the kid and feel like a good parent. That job wanted the opposite improvements — a smaller shake that finishes faster, so the parent isn't stuck waiting. Averaging the two jobs' requirements into one product would improve neither.

Four lessons, each of which recurs throughout this skill:

- **Circumstance beats customer characteristics.** The same person is a different customer at 7 a.m. than at 3 p.m. Demographics predicted nothing; the situation predicted everything.
- **One product gets hired for multiple jobs**, and you must know which job you are improving for.
- **Each job implies different improvements** — thicker and longer-lasting for the commute, smaller and quicker for the parent.
- **The competitive set is defined by the job**, and it includes things no category analysis would list.

## The two interpretations

JTBD is not one theory. It is two, sharing a vocabulary. Flattening them into mush is the most common expert-level error; hold them apart and use each for what it is good at.

### Jobs as progress (Christensen, Moesta, Klement)

A job is "the progress that a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance." Jobs on this view are **episodic**: they are born in a struggling moment, they generate a search, and they are resolved by hiring something. The unit of analysis is the **switch** — the causal story of how one real person moved from an old solution to a new one. Klement draws the strictest line: a JTBD is the struggle to transform a current life-situation into a preferred one when something blocks the way, and a job description **never contains a solution** — the moment a product or category name appears in the job, you have stopped describing demand and started describing supply.

### Jobs as activities (Ulwick, ODI)

A job is a task or process that a defined group of performers is trying to get done — "listen to music while traveling," "monitor a patient's vital signs." Jobs on this view are **stable** (the job outlives every solution), **mappable** (decomposable into an ordered sequence of steps), and **measurable** (each step generates desired outcomes — customer-defined metrics of success). A market is defined as a group of job performers plus a core functional job, and the win condition is explicit: help them get the job done better and/or more cheaply than the alternatives.

### Side by side

| Dimension | Jobs as progress | Jobs as activities |
|---|---|---|
| A job is | Progress sought in a circumstance | A task or process to get done |
| Lifespan | Episodic — born in struggle, resolved by a hire | Stable — outlives every solution |
| Unit of analysis | The switch (one real hiring story) | The job performer group + core functional job |
| Primary question | Why will someone switch? | What does "better" mean, measurably? |
| Core artifacts | Forces analysis, timeline, job stories | Job map, outcome statements, opportunity scores |
| Research instrument | Switch interview | Job mapping interview + quantified outcome survey |
| Characteristic failure | Unfalsifiable narrative | Optimizing the incumbent category |

### The real disagreements

Do not paper over these; they are substantive.

- The progress school charges that activity-decomposition **smuggles in the existing category**. Map the steps of "doing laundry" and you have assumed laundry; you will optimize the incumbent frame and never see the customer who solves the problem by buying clothes that don't need washing. Step-mapping tells you nothing about *why demand exists at all*, so it cannot explain or predict switching.
- The activities school charges that progress narratives are **unfalsifiable and unactionable**: any purchase can be retro-fitted with a progress story, two interviewers can extract two different "struggles" from the same customer, and a narrative about becoming a better parent does not tell an engineer which parameter to improve or a PM which backlog item wins.

Both charges are fair. Each school is weakest exactly where the other is strongest.

### The working synthesis this skill operates by

The schools answer different questions, so assign them by question, not by loyalty:

- Use **jobs-as-progress** to answer **WHY someone will switch**: demand generation, marketing, sales, positioning, churn, pricing psychology, "why isn't anyone buying."
- Use **jobs-as-activities** to answer **WHAT better means**: roadmap, specs, prioritization, segmentation by unmet need, "what should we build next."
- **Name the lens you are using** in any analysis you produce, so a reader can audit which school's assumptions you have imported. Mixing lenses silently is how JTBD work becomes unfalsifiable.

## Jobs-as-progress mechanics

### The struggling moment

All demand starts with a struggling moment: a circumstance where the current way of doing things stops being good enough and the gap becomes felt. No struggle, no demand — features do not create customers; struggling moments do. The practical consequence: when demand is the question, hunt for the struggling moment first, because everything else (forces, timeline, competitors) hangs off it.

### The four forces

Every switch is governed by four forces. Two promote change, two block it.

**Push of the situation** — dissatisfaction with the current state, generated by the circumstance itself, not by any new product.
- Example: a freelancer's invoice spreadsheet silently drops a row, and she spends three weeks chasing a payment she believed was sent.
- Predicts: whether anyone starts looking at all. No push, no search — and no amount of advertising substitutes for it.

**Pull of the new solution** — the attraction of the imagined better life with the new solution.
- Example: she watches a peer produce a numbered, tracked invoice in thirty seconds and pictures never chasing a payment again.
- Predicts: which direction a searcher moves and how much effort they invest. Pull attaches to a future self, not to a feature list.

**Anxiety of the new solution** — fear that blocks the switch. Always split it in two:
- **Anxiety-in-choice** — "which plan? will it handle my odd clients? what if I pick wrong?" Predicts stalls at the decision.
- **Anxiety-in-use** — "will importing my history mangle it? will I look incompetent while learning?" Predicts abandonment after purchase.

**Habit of the present** — attachment to and fluency with the current way of doing things, including workarounds.
- Example: "my spreadsheet is ugly, but I know exactly where everything is."
- Predicts: inertia even under strong push; this is the incumbent that must be fired before anything new can be hired.

**The switch condition: push + pull must outweigh anxiety + habit.** This gives you two distinct ways to win — amplify push and pull (demand generation) or reduce anxiety and habit friction (conversion). Reducing anxiety is usually cheaper than adding pull: a guarantee, a migration service, a trial, or a testimonial addressing a named fear typically costs less and moves more than another feature.

### The timeline: six stages of the demand-side journey

Every hire, traced backward, passes through six stages. Use them to locate where a prospect is and what force dominates there.

1. **First Thought** — something plants the idea that things could be different. "Without that first thought, there is no demand."
   Marketing that creates first thoughts creates markets; marketing aimed at people with no first thought is noise.
2. **Passive Looking** — noticing and learning with zero invested effort; solutions get registered, not compared.
   People can stay here for years until an event escalates the struggle into active search.
3. **Active Looking** — the searcher invests time: comparing, shortlisting, eliminating.
   Buying criteria form here, often shaped by whoever frames the trade-offs first.
4. **Deciding** — trade-offs get made, and anxiety-in-choice peaks. "People need to reject something before they can buy something else."
   Every purchase is also a firing; if nothing gets rejected, nothing gets bought.
5. **Onboarding** — the first use, measured against the expectations set at decision time.
   Whatever was overpromised in Deciding is collected here, with interest.
6. **Ongoing Use** — the product is re-hired at every use; this is where the jobs get done and progress is achieved.
   It is also where firing begins the moment the product disappoints.

### Big hire, little hire

The **big hire** is the purchase or signup; the **little hire** is choosing to use it again in each moment of need.
The big hire is won on promises; every little hire is won on delivery — which is why retention analysis is little-hire analysis.
Products that win big hires but lose little hires are churn machines; the timeline's last two stages are where they die.

### Hiring criteria

Hiring criteria are always functional AND emotional AND social — what it must do, how it must make the performer feel, and how it must make them look to others. On paper the functional criteria dominate; in real decisions the emotional and social criteria often cast the deciding vote. If your analysis of a hire has no emotional or social dimension, it is incomplete, not clean.

## Jobs-as-activities mechanics

### Market definition

A market is a group of **job performers** plus a **core functional job**: "parents (performers) trying to pass on family history to their children (job)" is a market; "the photo-book industry" is not. This definition is what makes ODI's promises possible — a stable market to size, map, and measure, unmoved by product churn.

### The core market ecosystem

Around any core functional job, distinguish the people involved, because each holds different needs:

- **Job executor** — the end user who performs the core functional job.
- **Purchase decision maker** — executes the **financial job**: evaluating and approving the buy. In consumer markets often the same person as the executor; in B2B almost never.
- **Product lifecycle support team** — everyone who performs **consumption chain jobs**: install, set up, store, transport, maintain, repair, clean, upgrade, dispose. Products lose deals and renewals on these jobs while teams polish the core one.

### Job types

- **Core functional job** — the market-defining job, stated solution-free as verb + object + contextual clarifier.
- **Related jobs** — adjacent jobs done alongside the core job. Serving several from one product is the platform play.
- **Emotional jobs** — how the performer wants to feel (personal dimension) and how they want to be perceived by others (social dimension — Ulwick folds social in here). They shape design and messaging even when the roadmap is functional.

### Needs as desired outcomes

ODI's central move is defining needs as **desired outcomes**: specially constructed metrics, one per way the performer measures success at a step of the job. Format: direction of improvement + performance metric + object of control + contextual clarifier — "minimize the time it takes to confirm the payment was received," "minimize the likelihood of overwatering plants while away." A fully mapped job typically yields 50–150 outcome statements, commonly 75 or more. Four properties make an outcome statement usable:

- **Measurable** — it names a metric (time, likelihood, number of errors) that can be surveyed and tracked.
- **Actionable** — an engineer or designer can attack it directly, without guessing at intent.
- **Solution-independent** — no product, feature, or technology appears in it, so it survives every solution generation.
- **Stable over time** — the same statements can be re-fielded years later, which makes them a durable innovation scorecard where feature requests are not.

### The job map

The core functional job decomposes into an eight-step universal structure: **Define, Locate, Prepare, Confirm, Execute, Monitor, Modify, Conclude**. Execute is the step everyone builds for; the money is usually in the neglected steps around it.

Applied to a generic job like "prepare a meal at home": define the menu, locate the ingredients, prepare the workspace, confirm everything is ready, execute the cooking, monitor doneness, modify seasoning as needed, conclude by serving and cleaning up. Full construction guidance and the step-by-step treatment are in references/formats.md.

### The Opportunity Algorithm

Performers rate each outcome for importance and satisfaction; **Opportunity = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)** on a 0–20 scale. High-importance, low-satisfaction outcomes are **underserved** (invest here); low-importance, high-satisfaction outcomes are **overserved** (cost-reduce or disrupt here); high-importance, high-satisfaction outcomes are **table stakes** (maintain parity, do not differentiate here). Scoring detail is in references/formats.md; the survey process is in references/research-methods.md.

### The Growth Strategy Matrix

Position a new product by whether it gets the job done better and what it charges:

- **Differentiated** — better but pricier; target an underserved segment.
- **Dominant** — better and cheaper; wins everyone.
- **Disruptive** — worse but cheaper; targets overserved customers or nonconsumers.
- **Discreet** — worse but available when options are constrained (the captive-context play).
- **Sustaining** — slightly better and/or slightly cheaper; the default incumbent move.

## Principles that hold across both schools

Whatever lens you are using, these hold. Treat a violation of any of them as a defect in the analysis, not a stylistic choice.

- **Jobs are stable; solutions are transient.** People needed to move money before banks and will after them. Strategy anchored on the job survives technology shifts; strategy anchored on the product dies with it.
- **State jobs solution-free.** A job containing a product name is a requirement in disguise.
- **Circumstance over characteristics.** Segment by situation, not by who people are.
- **The competitive set is defined by the job** — including workarounds, hacks, and doing nothing.
- **Every job has functional, emotional, and social dimensions.** Analyses missing the latter two are incomplete.
- **People hire progress, not products.** The purchase is a means; the progress is the end.
- **A new solution must defeat the incumbent's habit, not just beat it on features.** Better is necessary but not sufficient; switching costs and anxiety vote too.

## Misconceptions and traps

- **"JTBD is a task list."** Tasks are activity fragments; a pile of them with no struggle and no circumstance attached is a feature backlog in disguise.
  Correction: insist that every job carry a circumstance and a definition of progress, or refuse to call it a job.
- **"Jobs replace personas."** They replace personas as the *segmentation axis* — circumstance beats characteristics for predicting demand.
  Demographic and attitudinal detail can still inform tone, channels, and content; just never let it define the market.
- **"The job is what my product does."** Products are hires, not jobs, and restating your feature set in job grammar is self-flattery.
  Run the solution-swap test (references/artifact-analysis.md): if no rival solution could satisfy the "job," you wrote a product description.
- **"Customers can't tell you anything"** — the faux-Ford "faster horses" fatalism. Customers are unreliable about *solutions* but excellent witnesses to their own *struggle*.
  Interview for the struggle; design the solution yourself.
- **"JTBD is B2C only."** B2B jobs simply have more performers in the ecosystem — executor, purchase decision maker, support team — and more social and political emotional jobs ("don't let this purchase embarrass me").
  The machinery applies unchanged; the cast is bigger.
- **"One interview transcript = the job."** One story is an anecdote, not a job; any single teller may be idiosyncratic.
  Jobs stabilize as patterns across roughly 8–12 switch stories — before that, treat every stated job as a hypothesis.
- **"Everything is one giant job" / "every click is a job."** Both are altitude errors, in opposite directions.
  Hold the main job, little jobs, and outcomes apart as levels: a job you cannot decompose is too small; one that spans a whole life is too big to act on.
